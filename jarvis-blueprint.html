Feasibility study · Implementation plan

Project Jarvis Blueprint

Can one person build a working J.A.R.V.I.S. in 2026 with free software? Mostly yes. The voice, the brain, the memory and the hands are all buildable today with open-source parts. The holograms and the suit are not.

Researched 20 Oct 2026Target: Windows PC you already ownSoftware budget: $0
The short answer

What you can actually have

Buildable now, free

Say “Hey Jarvis”, talk naturally, get a spoken answer in a British voice, have it open apps, search the web, check weather, remember things about you, see through your webcam, and message you on your phone.

Buildable with limits

Speed and intelligence depend on your hardware. Free cloud AI is smarter but rate-limited and may train on your data. Whole-house listening needs cheap microphones you would have to buy.

Not possible today

Mid-air holograms you can touch, the Iron Man suit, a truly conscious AI, and “hacking into anything”. These are limits of physics, energy and law, not of your skill.

How a Jarvis works

Every voice assistant is the same seven-stage pipeline

Movie Jarvis feels like one magic mind. In practice it is a chain of small, separate programs. Each one is a free open-source component you can swap out later.

Side inputs feed stage 4 too: webcam (vision), a Telegram bot (your phone), and a scheduler (morning briefings, reminders).

Physics and limits

What stays in the movies, and what to build instead

Floating holograms you can touch

True mid-air holography is still experimental. Shipping “holographic” products are light-field screens and tables costing roughly $1K to $50K+, and experts put mainstream free-space holograms in the 2030s.

Instead: an animated HUD on a monitor or projector, or a Pepper’s-ghost reflection box built from glass and a phone.

The Iron Man suit

No battery stores enough energy in that size to fly a person, and nothing produces “repulsor” thrust. This is a physics limit, not a software one.

Instead: Jarvis on your phone and earbuds, which is the useful part of the suit.

A conscious, all-knowing AI

Current models predict text. They can be wrong with confidence, have no feelings, and know only what they were trained on or what your tools fetch.

Instead: give it good tools (web search, weather, calendar) so it looks things up instead of guessing.

Control over any system

Movie Jarvis hacks satellites and networks. A real assistant can only touch systems that offer an API you have permission to use.

Instead: connect your own accounts and devices, one tool at a time.

Recommended hybrid

Run ears, mouth and memory locally (fast, private, free forever). Use the free Gemini or Groq tier as the brain for hard questions, and fall back to a local Ollama model when you are offline or hit the daily limit. Keep private data such as passwords and documents on the local model only, since free cloud tiers may use your prompts for training.

Hardware

What your PC decides

You do not need to buy anything to start. Check what you have: open Task Manager → Performance and note your RAM and GPU memory.

Tier A

Laptop, no GPU

8–16 GB RAM · CPU only
  • Wake word, Whisper base/small, Kokoro or Piper: fine
  • Brain: use the free cloud tier
  • Local models of 1–4B params work but are slow and simple
Tier B

Gaming PC

NVIDIA GPU, 6–8 GB VRAM
  • Everything in Tier A, faster
  • 7–8B models locally at usable speed
  • Fully offline Jarvis becomes realistic
Tier C

Workstation

12–24 GB VRAM
  • 14–32B models locally, much smarter
  • Sub-second transcription
  • Local vision at good speed

Windows 10 stopped getting regular security updates in October 2025. Everything here still runs on it, but an assistant that controls your PC is safer on a supported system (Windows 11 or Linux) in the long run.

Implementation plan

Ten phases, about ten weeks part-time

Each phase ends with something that works. Do them in order: each one builds on the last, and you can stop at any phase and still have a useful assistant.

  1. Day 1–2
    PHASE 0 · WORKSHOP

    Set up your tools

    Install Python 3.11, Git, VS Code and Ollama. Make a project folder with a virtual environment. Get a free Gemini API key from Google AI Studio and store it in a .env file, never in your code.

    Done when ollama run answers a question and a 5-line Python script gets a reply from Gemini.
  2. Week 1
    PHASE 1 · BRAIN

    Text Jarvis in the terminal

    A chat loop with a system prompt that gives it the personality: polite, dry British wit, short answers. Keep conversation history so it follows context. Add automatic fallback from Gemini to Ollama when offline.

    google-genai · ollama · python-dotenv
    Done when you can hold a 10-message conversation and it remembers what you said earlier.
  3. Week 2
    PHASE 2 · EARS AND VOICE

    Push-to-talk conversation

    Hold a key, speak, release. Record with sounddevice, transcribe with faster-whisper, reply through Kokoro. Choose a British voice. Stream the voice sentence by sentence so it starts speaking before the full answer is ready.

    sounddevice · faster-whisper · kokoro
    Done when a spoken question gets a spoken answer in under about 3 seconds.
  4. Week 3
    PHASE 3 · ALWAYS LISTENING

    “Hey Jarvis” and interruptions

    Replace push-to-talk with openWakeWord’s hey_jarvis model. Use Silero VAD to tell when you stop talking. Let a second “Jarvis” cut it off mid-sentence. Play a short chime when it wakes.

    openwakeword · silero-vad
    Done when it wakes reliably from across the room and ignores the TV most of the time.
  5. Weeks 4–5
    PHASE 4 · HANDS

    Tools: make it do things

    Write small Python functions and register them for tool calling: time and date, weather (Open-Meteo), web search (DuckDuckGo), open apps and folders, volume, timers and reminders, system stats, play music, take a screenshot. Add a “confirm first” rule for anything that deletes, sends or spends.

    ddgs · requests · psutil · pyautogui · subprocess
    Done when “Open Chrome and search for flights to Mombasa” works by voice.
  6. Week 6
    PHASE 5 · MEMORY

    It knows who you are

    A remember tool writes facts to SQLite (“my sister is called Amina”). Save conversation summaries into Chroma and look up relevant ones before each answer. Load your profile into the system prompt at startup.

    sqlite3 · chromadb
    Done when you restart it and it still knows your name, preferences and yesterday’s reminders.
  7. Week 7
    PHASE 6 · EYES

    Camera and screen vision

    A look tool grabs a webcam frame or a screenshot and sends it to a vision model. Ask “what am I holding?” or “what’s this error on my screen?”.

    opencv-python · mss · Gemini vision or Ollama gemma3
    Done when it correctly describes an error message on your screen and suggests a fix.
  8. Week 8
    PHASE 7 · REACH

    Phone access and smart home

    A Telegram bot that forwards your texts and voice notes to the same brain, so you can use Jarvis away from home. Optionally connect Home Assistant if you buy smart plugs or bulbs later.

    python-telegram-bot · Home Assistant REST API
    Done when you text “what’s on my reminders?” from your phone and get an answer.
  9. Week 9
    PHASE 8 · FACE

    The HUD

    A FastAPI server pushes events over a WebSocket to a full-screen browser page: the glowing orb reacts to audio, plus transcript, tool log and system stats like the mockup above. Later, wrap it in Electron or Tauri as a desktop app that starts with Windows.

    fastapi · websockets · HTML/CSS/Canvas
    Done when the orb pulses while you talk and the tool log fills in live.
  10. Week 10
    PHASE 9 · INITIATIVE

    It speaks first

    A scheduler runs jobs on its own: morning briefing (weather, reminders, news headlines), “you’ve been coding for 3 hours” nudges, battery or disk warnings. This is what makes it feel like Jarvis rather than a chatbot.

    APScheduler
    Done when it greets you at 7 am with your day without being asked.
Ground rules

Keep it safe while it grows

  • Never let it run shell commands the model writes freely. Give it named tools with fixed behaviour instead.
  • Ask out loud before deleting files, sending messages or spending money, and log every tool call.
  • Keep API keys in .env and add that file to .gitignore before your first push to GitHub.
  • Route anything private (documents, passwords, health) to the local model only.
  • Free tier limits change often. Check each provider’s rate-limit page and build the Ollama fallback early.
